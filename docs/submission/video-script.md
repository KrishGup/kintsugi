# Demo video — what is in it

`docs/submission/kintsugi-demo.mp4` (5:38, 1280×720, under 10 MB). Narrated by Krish in one take; picture recorded on 14 Oct 2026
by `scripts/record_demo.py` from the first real GPT-6 session (`eval/reports/gpt6-first-run/`) on the private Azure instance.

How it was made: the take was transcribed with word timestamps (`whisper-1`); each transcript line was matched to its section of
the script, a section or line said twice keeps its last take, a section heading read aloud is dropped, cuts are snapped to the
silences in the waveform and pauses longer than 0.9 s are shortened to 0.6 s. The audio is high-passed, lightly denoised at the room's
measured floor, equalised from the take's own spectrum (the boxy 315–500 Hz bump and a gritty 5–6 kHz peak cut, presence
and air lifted), de-essed, lightly compressed and normalised to −16 LUFS in two passes. Playwright drives the real app for the
live shots; the cards are drafting-sheet slides; the captions are what was said, timed to the words. The run's numbers in the
narration (2 tries, the first did not compile, 15 checks, the critic's sign-off, about a dollar) match the session
(`python scripts/record_demo.py script --session <id>` warns when they do not).

What it does not show: a real phone photo going through intake (the example and the home screen describe that path; this run
started from a typed spec) and a printed part — neither exists yet.

| 0:00.0 | opener | card: title → live: overview | Hi, I'm Krish, and this is my hackathon project, Kintsugi. Kintsugi is the Japanese art of taking something that's broken and making it whole again. Today's world, we're too quick to throw things in the landfills that could easily be repaired. For this hackathon, it's the exact problem I wanted to solve. So I built Kintsugi, a web app built on top of OpenSCAD. It uses a node-based graph workflow to turn a few photos or a simple spec into a 3D printable repair. |
| 0:28.9 | why | card: why-1 → card: why-2 → card: why-3 → card: why-4 | 3D printing has come a long way. It's a lot cheaper now, and the printers are way better today than they were even three years ago. While the hardware has made huge advancements, the software hasn't really gotten any better. Modern CAD software is often bloated and very difficult to learn, and the open-source alternatives lack general adoption and frankly aren't any easier. Most AI 3D generation today just puts out a mesh. It might look right, but it's not built to exact measurements, and for repair, their measurements are the only thing that matters. OpenSCAD builds parts out of code, with exact numbers. It's difficult for a person to write, but it's perfect for an AI. So that's what Kintsugi builds on. |
| 1:15.3 | example | card: example → live: home | For example, let's say the handle on your water bottle snapped. The bottle itself is fine, but that small piece of plastic is broken. It's no reason to throw away the whole bottle. But how do you actually repair the handle? In the age of AI, this shouldn't be a difficult task. With Kintsugi, you print out a reference sheet, put the broken part on it, take a few pictures from a couple angles, and upload them, along with a quick sentence about what broke and an explanation of the part. |
| 1:47.7 | how it works | card: how-1 → card: how-2 → card: how-3 → card: how-4 → card: how-5 | From there, Kintsugi figures out what the part is and what it needs to fit onto. In this case, the neck of the bottle. It measures what it can, straight from the photos, using the markers in the reference sheet. If it's still missing something, it asks you. But only for the exact photo or measurement it needs, so it's not wasting your time. All of that turns into a spec. Every measurement the part has to hit, and how much room for error each one gets. GPT-6 designs the part in OpenSCAD, measures it, and checks its own work before you ever print anything. |
| 2:22.8 | the graph | live: graph | Under the hood, all of this runs on a node-based graph. Every step is a node, where each node says what it needs and what it produces. Compiling needs the code, the checks need the 3D model, so anything that doesn't depend on something else runs at the same time. The measurements and the drawing get built in parallel. And functionally, you're a node too. When it needs a photo or measurement from you, the graph just pauses there and picks back up when you answer. You can watch the whole thing run live in the Graph tab. |
| 2:58.1 | the demo | live: spec | For this demo, I'm starting from a spec instead of photos. If you already know your measurements, say you measured with calipers or a ruler, you can skip the photos and just type them in. The public demo runs a scripted version because I didn't want to leave my OpenAI key live on the internet. But the run you're about to see is a real one. |
| 3:17.8 | the run | live: timeline | GPT-6 is the orchestrator behind this entire thing. We feed it OpenSCAD's documentation directly, and it can write the parts and run the compile itself. Every dimension gets measured on the actual 3D mesh, not taken from its own code. So on this run, the first try didn't compile, but GPT-6 can read the error, fix it, and try again. And the second pass passed all fifteen checks. OpenSCAD is functionally a niche programming language, and I doubt there's much training data out there for this. So for a part like this, I think it did a really good job. |
| 3:54.1 | blueprints | live: sheets | This is one of my favorite parts. The blueprints. After every try, Kintsugi draws a full set of engineering drawings of what was actually built. Top, front, side, and cross sections, with the real measurements on them. Green means it's in tolerance, red means it's off. And these drawings are the model's reference, so it can keep iterating without re-scanning the photos or re-explaining everything to itself. It just looks where it's at. |
| 4:21.5 | checks + critic | live: checks | Every check here is a real measurement. Hover over one, and it shows you exactly where in the part it was measured. Then, a second GPT-6 reviews the drawings without ever seeing the code. Kind of like getting a second opinion from a coworker. On this run, it signed off. And this entire run, on a live endpoint, cost only a dollar. |
| 4:44.7 | customize | live: customize | The best thing about the result is that it's not a dead file. Every dimension has its own slider with a live 3D preview, and the ones that have to fit show where their number came from. So if you print it, and it's just slightly off, you can adjust the number and reprint. |
| 5:03.4 | coupon + export | live: export | A standard procedure for repairs like this is to print what's called a fit coupon first. It's basically just a slice of the part that has to fit, usually at three different sizes. So, it can print in a few minutes, you can see which one fits, and then you can print the real part. You get the STL, plus a print card with the settings. |
